## Build for a live, one-pass experience

The audience cannot reread a spoken sentence. Design for comprehension, energy, silence, and memory.

## Classify the talk

- Thought leadership
- Technical teaching
- Case study
- Product or launch keynote
- Panel or fireside remarks
- Lightning talk
- Closing or inspirational talk

Use the content job to determine the arc.

## Talk state

Record:

```text
session length
audience and room
speaker relationship to topic
audience starting belief
one-sentence thesis or learning outcome
opening tension
major beats
receipts or demonstrations
participation moments
energy changes
ending movement
```

## Arc for belief-changing talks

```text
specific opening tension
-> audience recognition
-> surprising observation
-> mechanism
-> receipts
-> strongest objection
-> limitation
-> new decision rule
-> escalating ending
```

## Arc for teaching talks

```text
useful task or failure
-> mental model
-> demonstration
-> practice or prediction
-> failure modes
-> decision guidance
-> next capability
```

Do not add contrarian drama when the audience mainly needs a working model.

## Opening

Open with a claim, event, failure, demonstration, audience observation, or decision.

Avoid:

- Long credentials
- Generic thanks as the first substantive minute
- "Today I am going to talk about"
- A broad industry landscape
- A question whose answer is obvious
- An inspirational promise before trust is earned

Establish why this speaker, this audience, and this moment belong together.

## Spoken-language pass

- Use sentences the speaker can say in one breath.
- Keep the subject and verb visible.
- Prefer familiar words when technical precision is unchanged.
- Remove nested clauses that require rereading.
- Let an important line stand without immediately paraphrasing it.
- Mark pauses, demonstrations, audience responses, and slide changes separately.
- Read numbers in a form the audience can process.
- Avoid administrative transitions such as "moving on to the next section."

Read the script aloud or simulate a read-aloud pass. Rewrite anything that sounds authored rather than spoken.

## Timing

Estimate spoken time from actual words, not slide count.

Use a default range of 120 to 145 words per minute, then reserve time for:

- Laughter or reaction
- Audience participation
- Demonstrations
- Pauses
- Slide transitions
- Questions, when included

A 30-minute slot should not contain 30 minutes of uninterrupted script.

## Stories

Use a story when it changes the claim, reveals a mechanism, or earns the emotion.

For each story, record:

```text
why this story exists
specific event
choice or conflict
what changed
what the audience can transfer
```

Do not invent personal experience or polish away the uncertainty that made the story credible.

## Audience participation

Use participation only when it helps the audience notice, choose, predict, diagnose, or commit.

Define:

```text
prompt
expected action
time needed
what the response reveals
what happens if nobody responds
```

Do not use "raise your hand if" merely to add interactivity.

## Humor

Use self-aware or situational humor. Do not write a joke quota. Keep humor subordinate to the claim.

## Ending

The ending should perform one final movement:

- Resolve the opening image
- Reframe the next decision
- Reveal a consequence
- Move from technical mechanism to human stake
- Give one exact action

Do not end with a generic recap followed by "Thank you."

## Deliverable modes

### Full script

Return spoken lines, stage directions, slide cues, and timing markers in clearly separate forms.

### Speaker notes

Return beat-level notes with exact lines only where wording matters.

### Talk architecture

Return the thesis, audience movement, timed beats, evidence plan, participation, and ending. Use only when the user asks for an outline or architecture rather than a full script.
